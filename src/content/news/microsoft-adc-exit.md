In October 2026, Microsoft confirmed the closure of its Nairobi Africa Development Centre — the $100M engineering hub opened in 2019 as a flagship bet on Kenyan engineering talent. The immediate effect is a pool of several hundred senior engineers, product managers and designers re-entering the market at once, in a half-year when venture funding has already tightened 44.5%.

For startups, this is the cheapest senior-talent moment Nairobi has offered in years. The engineers the ADC trained — distributed systems, data platforms, developer tooling — map almost exactly onto what Kenyan startups at Series A say they cannot hire: payment-rails backend engineers, data scientists who can ship, platform engineers who can hold uptime on thin infrastructure budgets.

The risk is retention, not supply. The ADC's closure also signals to global tech that Nairobi is a variable-cost location, not a commitment — and the same engineers being courted by startups are being courted by remote employers abroad paying dollar salaries. Local companies competing on mission and equity will need to be honest about what they can pay; the market bands in our compensation guide are a starting point, not a ceiling.

The policy question lands with the government and the county: the ecosystem's pitch for the next decade cannot be cheap labour — it has to be the quality of the pipeline, and the pipeline needs the institutions (universities, certification, broadband) that keep producing engineers worth hiring at global wages.
